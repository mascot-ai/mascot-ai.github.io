> **TL;DR**
>
> - Sort a school by two numbers from its own data: the admit rate and the middle 50% range of grades and test scores for admitted students.
> - A low admit rate makes a school a reach for almost everyone, however strong the record.
> - A real safety is three things at once: you are very likely to get in, your family can afford it, and you would be glad to go.
> - Your sort is a starting guess. Update it when your grades change or a school's numbers do.

Every college list guide tells you to have reach, target, and safety schools. Few say how to decide which is which. The labels sound official, but there is no standard formula. You sort schools with a few public numbers and some honesty about your own record. This post walks through that process. For how the three groups fit into a whole list, start with [How to build a balanced college list](/blog/how-to-build-a-balanced-college-list/).

## What do reach, target, and safety actually mean?

A reach is a school where admission is uncertain even for a strong applicant. A target is a school where your record looks like that of the students it usually admits. A safety is a school where you are very likely to be admitted and can afford to attend.

The labels describe your odds at one school, so the same college can be a reach for one student and a target for another. A school that admits most of its applicants might be a safety for a student with a 3.9. A school that admits one applicant in eight is a reach for everybody, including the student with a 4.0.

## Which numbers do you need for each school?

You need four, and all of them are public:

1. The admit rate, the share of applicants the school admitted last cycle.
2. The middle 50% of GPA, if the school reports it.
3. The middle 50% of test scores, if you plan to send them. That is the range between the 25th and 75th percentile of admitted students who submitted scores.
4. The net price, which is what a family like yours pays after grants and scholarships.

Look on the school's admissions page, in its Common Data Set (many colleges post one, and Section C covers admissions), and in the federal College Navigator tool at nces.ed.gov/collegenavigator. If the sources disagree, trust the school's most recent Common Data Set and note the year.

Two cautions. Test-optional schools report scores only for students who sent them, and those students tend to score higher than the full admitted class, so the range can make a school look harder to get into than it is. And a GPA range only helps if you know how the school reads your grades, since weighting and course rigor differ from one high school to the next.

## How do you sort a school, step by step?

Run every school through the same three checks, in this order.

Check 1 is the admit rate. If the school admits a small fraction of its applicants, call it a reach and stop. When thousands of students with top grades are turned away each year, your record cannot lift the odds enough to call it a target. A rough rule of thumb is that anything under about 20 percent is a reach for nearly everyone. That cutoff is a guideline, not an official line.

Check 2 compares your numbers with the middle 50%. For schools that pass Check 1:

- Below the 25th percentile: reach.
- Between the 25th and 75th percentile: target.
- Above the 75th percentile: target or safety, depending on Check 3.

Check 3 is the safety test. A school counts as a safety only if all three of these are true:

- You are above the 75th percentile and the school admits a large share of its applicants.
- You can afford it. Look at the net price, not the sticker price.
- You would be glad to attend, including for the major you want.

A school that fails any one of these is not a safety, however likely the acceptance letter is. A cheap school you would hate leaves you in the same spot as having no safety at all.

## What does this look like for a real student?

Take a made-up student, Dana, and three made-up schools. The numbers are illustrative, not from any real college. Dana has a 3.7 unweighted GPA and a 1280 SAT.

| School | Admit rate | Middle 50% SAT | Net price for Dana | Sort |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| College A | 11% | 1450 to 1560 | $28,000 | Reach |
| College B | 58% | 1180 to 1360 | $22,000 | Target |
| College C | 82% | 1020 to 1230 | $9,000 | Safety |

College A fails Check 1 on admit rate alone, and Dana's 1280 is also below its 25th percentile, so there are two reasons to call it a reach. College B is a target: the admit rate is middling and 1280 sits inside the range. College C passes the first two checks easily and the net price is low. If Dana would be glad to go and it offers the major she wants, it is a safety.

Now change one thing. If College C's net price were $31,000 and Dana's family could not cover it, College C would stop being a safety. Dana would get in and be unable to attend. The sort depends on money as much as on scores.

## What mistakes make a sort wrong?

- Treating a school's reputation as your odds. The admit rate sets the reach label, not name recognition.
- Calling a school a safety because its admit rate is high. A high rate does not make you safe. A school that admits most applicants can still turn down a student who sits below its range.
- Ignoring the program. Some schools admit by major or division, and engineering or nursing can be much harder to enter than the college overall. Check the program's admit rate if the school publishes it.
- Using last year's numbers as a promise. Rates and ranges move every cycle.
- Counting a school you would not attend. If you would rather take a gap year than go, it is not a safety.

## Checklist: is this school sorted right?

Before a school goes on your list, answer yes or no.

- I found the admit rate in the school's own data.
- I compared my GPA and scores to the middle 50%, not just the average.
- I checked the net price with the school's calculator.
- If I labeled it a safety, I would be glad to go.
- I know which year the numbers come from.

Any "no" means the label is a guess. Go get the number.

## What should you do when a school sits between two groups?

Some schools fall on the line. Your GPA is above the range and your score is below it, or the admit rate is 25 percent and you land in the middle of the range. Treat these as the more selective label. Calling a borderline school a target and being wrong costs more than calling it a reach and being pleasantly surprised.

The decision rule: when in doubt, sort one level harder. A borderline target becomes a reach, and a borderline safety becomes a target. That keeps your list from leaning on a school that was never as certain as it looked.

## How often should you re-sort?

Whenever something changes: a new semester of grades, a test score, a school's updated Common Data Set, or a shift in what your family can pay. Schools move between groups more often than students expect. A strong junior year can lift a target to a safety, and a lower net price can turn an unaffordable school into a real option.

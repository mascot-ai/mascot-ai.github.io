> **TL;DR**
>
> - Most bad Aprils trace back to a list problem that was visible in the fall: no affordable safety, too many reaches, or schools nobody checked.
> - The nine mistakes below each have a symptom you can spot today and a fix that takes under an hour.
> - If you fix only one, fix the safety: it must be a school you would attend and can pay for.
> - Re-check the list after your fall grades and before every deadline.

Regular decision answers come in from mid-March into early April. By then the list is locked. Whatever was wrong with it in October is now a letter, and you cannot change it.

The good news is that these problems are easy to see early. Below are nine mistakes counselors run into again and again, what each one looks like on a real list, and how to fix it while you still can. For the full method of building a list, start with [How to build a balanced college list](/blog/how-to-build-a-balanced-college-list/).

## What are the nine college list mistakes?

Each item has the same three parts: how it plays out, how to spot it now, and the fix.

### 1. Your safety costs too much

**How it plays out:** you are admitted everywhere that was "safe" and cannot afford any of it.

**Spot it:** you have not run the net price calculator on your safeties. Colleges that take federal aid must post one on their website. It takes about ten minutes per school.

**Fix:** run it at every safety. The U.S. Department of Education's [College Scorecard](https://collegescorecard.ed.gov/) shows average net price by income band if you want a first look. If the net price is more than your family can pay, the school is not a safety. Look for a public university in your state, or a school that advertises automatic merit aid for your grades.

### 2. Your safety is a school you would not attend

**How it plays out:** you are admitted to a safety and feel dread, not relief.

**Spot it:** ask yourself, "If this were my only acceptance, would I go?" If the honest answer is a gap year, it is not a safety.

**Fix:** swap it for a school you can picture yourself at. Two real safeties beat four you added to fill a slot.

### 3. The list is mostly reaches

**How it plays out:** a stack of rejections and one or two acceptances you did not plan around.

**Spot it:** sort every school by its admit rate and by where your grades and scores sit against its published range. If more than a third of the list is reaches, it is top-heavy. A reach turns away most applicants, even strong ones, so no number of them adds up to a safe outcome.

**Fix:** keep the reaches you love and add targets until you have at least as many targets as reaches. [Reach, target, and safety](/blog/reach-target-safety-schools/) shows the sort, step by step.

### 4. You applied binding early decision without checking the price

**How it plays out:** you are admitted in December to a school you are committed to, and the aid offer is not enough.

**Spot it:** early decision is binding. You can usually leave only if the aid offer cannot cover the cost, and each school handles that its own way.

**Fix:** run the net price calculator before you commit to a binding round. If the number scares you, apply early action or regular decision instead.

### 5. You looked at the college, not the program

**How it plays out:** you are in the university but not in the major you wanted.

**Spot it:** some schools admit you to a specific college or division, and engineering, nursing, and computer science can be much harder to enter than the school overall. Look on the admissions page for the program's own numbers and rules about changing majors.

**Fix:** for any school that admits by program, treat the program's odds as the school's odds. Write down whether changing majors later is easy, hard, or not allowed.

### 6. You never checked what each school requires

**How it plays out:** in January you drop a school because its supplement, test policy, or interview rule turns out to be more than you have time for.

**Spot it:** you cannot say, for each school, how many essays it asks for, whether it wants test scores, and when the deadline is.

**Fix:** build a one-line row per school with essay count, test policy, and deadline. Do this once, in the fall, and cut a school if the work does not match your interest.

### 7. You missed the scholarship deadline

**How it plays out:** you get in and the aid is thinner than a friend's at the same school.

**Spot it:** some schools set an earlier deadline for merit scholarships or honors programs than for admission, and the application may not say so.

**Fix:** put the scholarship deadline next to the admission deadline in each school's row. Check the school's financial aid page, not just the admissions page.

### 8. You built the list around one dream school

**How it plays out:** the dream school says no and you have not looked closely at the others.

**Spot it:** you can write three paragraphs about one school and one sentence about the rest.

**Fix:** write a real reason for every school, in your own words, before it counts. If you cannot, it is padding. See [how many colleges you should actually apply to](/blog/how-many-colleges-should-you-apply-to/) for where the number should land.

### 9. You stopped updating the list

**How it plays out:** the list is a snapshot from the spring of junior year and no longer fits you.

**Spot it:** your fall grades came in, your interests shifted, or a school changed a policy, and the list did not move.

**Fix:** re-sort the list after fall grades and before each deadline. The list is allowed to change as you learn. If you are still in junior year, [the junior-year plan](/blog/build-college-list-junior-year/) shows when to re-check.

## What does this look like for a real student?

Take a made-up student, Marcus, with a 3.6 unweighted GPA, interest in nursing, and a family budget of about $15,000 a year. The schools below are invented for the example.

| Group | Schools | Problem |
|---|---|---|
| Reach | 5 | Mostly well-known private schools |
| Target | 1 | A state university |
| Safety | 1 | A private college with a net price near $32,000 |

Marcus has three of the mistakes at once. The list is top-heavy (mistake 3), his one safety costs double his budget (mistake 1), and he has not checked whether his reaches admit nursing students directly (mistake 5).

His fix takes one evening. He runs the calculator at the safety, finds it fails, and replaces it with an in-state public school that has a nursing program and a net price near $13,000. He drops two of the five reaches, adds two targets that admit nursing students directly, and writes one honest reason per school. The list now has three reaches, three targets, and two safeties.

## Checklist: is your list ready for April?

- I ran the net price calculator at every school, and my safeties fit my budget.
- I would attend every school on the list.
- I have at least as many targets as reaches.
- I checked the program's admit rate at every school that admits by major.
- I know the essay count, test policy, and deadline for each school.
- I know the scholarship deadline for each school that offers merit aid.
- I have a real reason for every school, in my own words.
- I will re-sort the list after my fall grades.

## What is a simple rule for checking your list?

Run one test on each school: would you attend it, and can you pay for it? A school that fails either part is not a safety. A list with at least two schools that pass both is one you can get through April with, whatever the reaches do.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is it too late to fix my list if I am already a senior?

No. If you have not submitted, you can still swap schools. Check each school's deadline first, because a swap that needs a new supplement has to fit in the time you have left.

### How many safeties do I really need?

Two is a good floor. They give you a backup if an aid offer surprises you or a school changes a policy.

### What if every school I love is a reach?

Keep the ones you love, then add targets you can be excited about. A list can be ambitious and still protect you.

### Do I need to apply to more than eight schools?

Not always. The [Common App](https://www.commonapp.org/) allows up to twenty (check its current limit), but a longer list is not a better one. Eight to twelve schools with strong applications usually beats twenty rushed ones.

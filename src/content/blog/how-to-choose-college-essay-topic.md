> **TL;DR**
>
> - The personal statement is one essay, up to 650 words, that every Common App college on your list will see. Its job is to show how you think, not to list what you did.
> - A topic works when it is specific, genuinely yours, and leaves you room to reflect. The subject itself can be small.
> - No subject is banned. Overdone topics fall flat because of a generic takeaway, not because of the topic.
> - Find your topic by noticing the moments you keep returning to, then asking "so what" until you reach something only you could say.
> - Choosing the topic and writing the essay are two separate jobs. This guide is about the first one.

Most students spend more time stuck on the topic than stuck on the essay. The blank page is not really the problem. The problem is not knowing which of your experiences is worth 650 words, and worrying that you do not have one.

In advising sessions, the topic stall reliably eats more time than the drafting does. Most students show up with three or four usable threads they have already talked themselves out of for being too small.

There is a lot of advice about this, and a fair amount of it is wrong or out of date. You have probably heard that certain subjects are off limits, that readers are tired of certain stories, or that there is a formula that works. Some of that has a grain of truth in it. Most of it will send you chasing a topic that is not yours.

This guide covers what the personal statement is for, what makes a topic work, which topics tend to give students trouble and why, how to handle the fear that your idea is overdone, and a short brainstorm you can sit down and do this week.

One thing to be clear about up front. Choosing what to write about and writing the thing are different jobs. This guide is about the first one. When you finish it, you should know how to decide on a topic. The drafting, the structure, and the cutting all come after, and that is a separate process.

## What the personal statement is actually for

Start with the mechanics. On the Common App, the personal statement can run up to 650 words, with a stated minimum of 250, and the same essay goes to every Common App college on your list. There are seven prompts to choose from, and they are unchanged for the 2026-2027 cycle. Before you start, confirm the current word limit and prompt list on the [Common App essay prompts page](https://www.commonapp.org/apply/essay-prompts), since that page is the source that actually gets updated.

By the time a reader opens your essay, they already have your transcript, the rigor of your courses, your test scores if you sent them, and your activities list. Those parts tell them what you did. The essay is where they get a sense of how you think, what you notice, and how you handle something that did not go to plan.

It matters, but it sits below the academic record. In [NACAC's most recent State of College Admission report](https://nacacnet.org/wp-content/uploads/2022/10/soca2019_ch3.pdf), published in 2019 using data from a 2017 survey of admissions offices, about a quarter of colleges said the essay carried "considerable importance" in the decision, well behind grades in college-prep courses and the strength of a student's curriculum. That is the most recent published version of that data, so treat it as a direction rather than a current measurement. The essay is closer to a tie-breaker and a character check than a lever.

The reason the topic has to do its work quickly is the sheer volume readers are moving through. [Higher Ed Dive](https://www.highereddive.com/news/common-app-data-shows-5-jump-in-first-year-college-applicants/737732/), reporting on Common App data, put the count at roughly 6.7 million applications from more than 1.2 million first-year applicants through January 1, 2025, up about 5 percent from the year before and around 40 percent over four cycles. A reader is going through a stack of essays in one sitting. Yours does not get a slow, generous read on the first pass.

You may have seen a specific number for how long a reader spends on each essay, often "four minutes" or an "eight-minute rule." There is no reliable current figure for this. The numbers that circulate are either unsourced or trace back to old journalism about a single office, and admissions reading has changed a great deal since then. The honest version is just that the first read is quick, so the opening and the topic have to carry.

So the job of the topic is narrow. Give a quick reader a clear view of how you think, using something specific enough that it could only have come from you.

## What makes a topic work

Three things tend to be true of topics that go well. You can run an idea through them before you commit.

### It is specific

A topic is not a theme. "I learned resilience from soccer" is a category that thousands of applicants could write under. "The season I kept the scorebook for a team I used to start on" is a topic. The second one has a scene, a particular position you were in, and a tension to work with. Concrete detail is also the part a reader remembers, and it is the part nobody else can reproduce, because they were not there.

### It is actually yours

The essay should sound like you and be about you. MIT Admissions, in its [guidance on writing the college essay](https://mitadmissions.org/blogs/entry/how-to-write-a-college-essay/), tells students to make the essay "yours" and to avoid "buzzwords and banality," the language students reach for when they are writing what they think a committee wants. A topic fails this test when you picked it for how it sounds, or when the voice on the page is a stiff, official version of you. Read a paragraph out loud. If you would never say it that way to a person, that is a sign.

### It leaves you room to reflect

Run this check on a candidate topic. After you have described what happened, could you keep going for another 250 or 300 words about what you made of it, without repeating yourself. If the topic is only an event, and there is nothing underneath it, the essay runs out early and starts to pad. The strongest topics are the ones where the thing that happened is small and the thinking afterward is large.

An example. "I won the regional debate final" is an event with a lid on it. Once you have described the round and the result, you are done, and the rest of the essay has to strain to find a lesson. "I kept arguing positions I did not believe, and one day it started to bother me" is the same activity with the lid off. There is a whole essay in the second one, because the interesting part is a change in how you think, and that is exactly what a reader is trying to see.

## Topics that tend to fall flat

None of these is banned. Every one of them has produced essays that worked. They show up here because readers watch them go wrong more often than not, usually for a reason you can fix.

### The activities list in sentence form

If the essay walks through your extracurriculars in full paragraphs, it is using 650 words to restate a section the reader already has. The application tells them you were captain, or first chair, or the founder of the club. The essay is the place to pick one moment inside that and show what it asked of you.

### It ends up being about someone else

Essays about a grandparent, a coach, a parent, or a friend who went through something hard can work, but only if the reader finishes knowing more about you than about the other person. A good check is to cut every sentence that is purely a description of them. If there is almost nothing left, the topic is really a portrait of that person, and it belongs in a different kind of writing.

### It is something you are still in the middle of

Hard experiences can make strong essays, usually once there is some distance and you have had time to make sense of it. If writing about it is still painful, or you do not yet know what you think about it, it is probably too soon. That is not a rule about subject matter. It is about whether you can currently do anything with it on the page.

### You picked it to impress

Readers can usually tell when a student is writing about the prestigious internship, the award, or the trip because they believe that is what admissions wants. The writing goes flat because the interest is not real. A plain topic you actually care about will beat an impressive one you do not.

The pattern across all four is the same. The subject is rarely the problem. The reflection is. [College Essay Guy](https://www.collegeessayguy.com/blog/what-not-to-write-college-essay) makes this point with the classic sports-injury essay, which "fails when it's about the injury" and "succeeds when it's about the identity crisis that followed." A line often attributed to MIT Admissions puts it more bluntly, that there are no trite topics, only trite answers. Whether or not that exact phrasing is theirs, it is the right idea. Fix the thinking and most "bad" topics are fine.

## The overdone-topic question

More students abandon a good idea over this than over any other worry. You have a topic, then you read a list somewhere that says it has been done to death, and you drop it. Here is the honest version.

Certain topics genuinely do recur, year after year, and admissions people say so openly. Across write-ups from Georgia Tech, College Essay Guy, and others, the same ones come up: the sports injury or the big game, the service trip or mission trip, the immigrant-family story or the grandparent's sacrifice, the death of a relative, moving to a new place, and the "my hero is my parent" essay. If you are writing one of these, a reader has very likely seen several versions of it that same week.

That is not disqualifying. Rick Clark, who runs admissions at Georgia Tech, said in a [2022 piece for Grown and Flown](https://grownandflown.com/director-admission-college-essays/) that "knowing the topic won't differentiate you" and that "your voice has to be evident" instead. He also made the point that "there is no utterly unique topic." Nobody is holding out for a subject that has never been written about, because it does not exist. What separates two essays on the same event is the specific detail and the specific thinking.

The practical test is this. Ask whether your version could have been written by a hundred other applicants who went through the same thing. If the answer is yes, the problem is not the topic, it is that you have not reached the part only you can write. Add the specific moment and the specific reaction that the other ninety-nine would not have had. If you cannot find that part, then it may be worth picking something else, especially if you were half-drawn to it because it sounded serious.

A few of the common ones tend to split like this. The left column is where the essay goes generic. The right column is what the version that works is usually really about.

| Common topic | Where it goes generic | What the version that works is usually about |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Sports injury or the big game | The play-by-play, then a line about perseverance | Who you were without the sport, and what you rebuilt |
| Service or mission trip | What the place taught you about gratitude | A specific assumption you brought and had to drop |
| Grandparent or immigrant-family story | Their sacrifice, told as biography | A value you inherited and what you do with it now |
| Death of a relative | The loss itself, described in full | A small habit or choice that changed afterward |
| Moving to a new place | Feeling like an outsider, then adjusting | One relationship or routine that shifted how you fit in |
| "My hero is my parent" | A tribute to everything they do | One thing they got wrong, or one thing you now see differently |

## How to actually find your topic

The search works better if you stop looking for your most impressive experience and start looking for the ones your mind keeps going back to.

### Notice what you return to

Pay attention to the memories that resurface on their own: a conversation that changed your mind, a job that was stranger than you expected, something you got wrong and still think about. These are worth more than the milestones, because you have already been reflecting on them without trying. The essay is mostly a matter of writing that reflection down.

### Small is fine

Good essays are often about something that sounds like nothing. Johns Hopkins publishes a set of ["Essays That Worked"](https://apply.jhu.edu/college-planning-guide/essays-that-worked/) each year, and the through-line, in their words, is students who "share stories from their everyday lives to reveal something about their character, values, and life." A walk you take, a thing you collect, a regular customer at the place you work, an ongoing argument with a sibling. MIT's admissions bloggers make a similar point about noticing the "understated" moments rather than only the leadership titles. The size of the subject does not matter. What you saw in it does.

### Run each idea through "so what"

Take a candidate topic and describe what happened in a few sentences. Then ask: so what? What changed in how you see things? What would a reader understand about you that is not already on the application? If you have a real answer, you have a topic. If the honest answer is "nothing really, it was just a good day," keep it on the list but keep looking.

### Do not start from the prompts

The seven Common App prompts are broad on purpose, and one of them ends with "topic of your choice." They are a finishing step, not a starting point. Pick what you want to write about first, using the moments and the "so what" test above, then look down the list and find the prompt your topic already answers. There is almost always one that fits, and if the only match is "topic of your choice," that is a real option and no reader holds it against you. Choosing a prompt and then hunting for a memory that fits it is how students end up with a topic they do not care about.

## A short brainstorming session you can do this week

Block out thirty or forty minutes, somewhere you can talk out loud or write without editing. The goal is not a finished topic. It is a shortlist of threads.

Work through these questions. Say the answers out loud if you can, and listen for the ones where you sound like yourself rather than like a form. Rick Clark's read-it-aloud advice applies at the idea stage too, not only to a finished draft.

- What is something you changed your mind about in the last two years, and what moved you?
- What do you do with a free afternoon and no plans?
- What is a small thing you are unusually particular about, and why?
- When did you last feel out of place, and what did you do next?
- What would someone who has only seen your transcript get wrong about you?
- What is a question or problem you keep coming back to even though no one assigned it?
- What are you good at that has no award attached to it?
- What is a small exchange with someone that stuck with you, and what did it change in how you act?
- What is a time you were wrong and had to sit with it?

When you are done, circle two or three that you kept talking about after the question was over. Run each one through the "so what" test from the section above. One of them is usually a draft.

## If you are a parent

The rest of this guide is written to the student. This part is for you.

You can help most by being a memory. Students forget their own material: the summer job that went sideways, the thing they built when they were twelve, the way they handled a hard stretch last year. Remind them of moments, then step back. You can also be the person they talk it through with, and the question worth asking is "what did you make of that," not "is that impressive enough."

What does not help is choosing the topic for them, vetoing a small idea because it does not sound weighty, or editing sentences until the essay sounds like you wrote it. The most common overreach is that last one. Admissions readers know what a seventeen-year-old sounds like, and they catch adult-sounding sentences fast. An essay that clearly had a parent's hand on the wording tends to work against the student, not for them. The essay is one of the few parts of the application that is entirely theirs. Keep it that way.

## What to do once you have a topic

A topic and a page of notes is the finish line for this guide. From here, the work changes. Drafting, deciding which prompt to file it under, finding a structure, and cutting it to length are separate tasks. The one piece of advice that carries over is to write the first version quickly and badly, then fix it. You cannot edit a blank page.

A useful page of notes has the concrete moment written down in plain detail, a few sentences on what you thought at the time, a few more on what you think now, and one line on what you want a reader to understand about you. That is enough to start. If your notes are only a summary of the event with no thinking attached, go back to the "so what" test before you draft, because that gap does not close on its own once you start writing.

This is also the part of [Mascot](/features/) that runs as a conversation. You talk through your experiences out loud, it helps you hear which thread has something behind it, and you come away with a topic and notes, not a draft. It does not write the essay. That part is yours.

## Keeping this in perspective

The topic question feels larger than it is. You are not hunting for a story no one has ever told, or for the single most dramatic thing that has happened to you. You are looking for something true, something specific, and something you still have thinking left to do about. Most students I work with already have three or four of those. The real work is noticing them, and then being willing to write plainly about what they meant.

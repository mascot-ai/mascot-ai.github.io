> **TL;DR**
>
> - A good "why us" essay joins one specific thing about you to one specific, checkable thing about the school.
> - The test: if you could swap in another college's name and the essay still works, it is not done.
> - Research the school for specifics before you write, not after.
> - Start from what you want out of the next four years, not from the school's marketing.

The "why this college" supplement is the essay students most often write on autopilot. The reason is simple: the easy facts are the ones everybody finds first. Rankings, a pretty quad, a good location, a "vibrant community." They are true of the school and true of a dozen others, so the essay reads like a brochure with your name on it.

What the college is really asking is narrower. Why would *you*, specifically, use *this* place? That takes two halves: something real about you, and something real about the school. This post is about finding the match between them. It is not about drafting. For how this essay differs from your personal statement and the other supplement types, see [Personal statement vs supplemental essays: different topics, different jobs](/blog/personal-statement-vs-supplemental-essays/).

## The swap test

Take your draft, or even just your list of reasons, and replace the college's name with another school's. If the sentences still make sense, they are not doing the job. That is the whole test, and it catches most weak drafts.

Run it on a hypothetical. Say a mid-sized college has a co-op program that places students in paid, full-time work for a semester. A typical first attempt looks like this:

*"I am excited about the college's co-op program, which will give me real-world experience. Combined with its beautiful campus and supportive community, I know it is the right place for me."*

Swap in any school with a co-op program, or really any school at all, and nothing breaks. Now a version that passes:

*"For four years I have learned sign language to talk with my deaf cousin, and I want to know what it takes to work with people who communicate differently than I do. A co-op semester in a hospital audiology clinic would let me find out before I commit to a major."*

Swap in another school and this one breaks. It hangs on one specific co-op offering and on something particular about the student. (Both examples are illustrative, and the college is not a real one.) The personal half is doing real work here. Take it away and you are back to the brochure.

Use the swap test early and often. It is quicker than rereading a full draft and more honest than asking a friend whether it "sounds good."

## Start with what you want, not with the college

Most students do this in the wrong order. They open the college's website, collect the nicest-sounding features, and then try to explain why those features matter to them. You end up reverse-engineering enthusiasm.

Flip it. Before you look at the school again, write down three things you want from the next four years. Make them concrete. "A great education" does not count, and neither does "opportunities." Something like these does:

- I want to try a field I have never had a class in, without it wrecking my schedule.
- I want to spend time working outside a classroom before I graduate.
- I want a professor who will let me chase a question that is not on the syllabus.

Yours will differ from these, and from your friends' lists. If you have trouble getting to three, think about what has been frustrating or too small in high school. Wants often hide in complaints. Then think about the last few things you did without anyone making you, and what you were hoping to get out of them.

Now go to the school and look for where it offers those things. Some wants will find nothing. Fine, drop them. Some will find several matches. Pick the one that connects most clearly to something you have actually done or cared about for a while. That is your angle.

This changes how you read the site. You stop scanning it hoping to be impressed and start asking whether a particular place can give you a particular thing, which is how a real decision about where to spend four years should go anyway.

## Where to find specifics that are actually checkable

A checkable detail is one a reader could confirm on the college's own site in a minute. Vague praise cannot be checked. A named program, a course, an office, or a requirement can. The college's own materials are the best place to look, because they are current and they are written for exactly this purpose.

Places worth your time:

- The course catalog, especially upper-level courses in a field you are curious about
- Department pages, which often describe how the major is structured and what students do in it
- Student organization lists, to see what exists and what looks inactive
- Advising and career services pages, which show how the school supports students outside class
- Study-abroad and co-op or internship listings
- Faculty research pages, for what professors are working on now and whether undergraduates are involved

Verify anything you plan to mention against the school's current website before you use it. Programs get renamed, courses get dropped, and an old forum post or a friend's memory is not a source. If a detail turns out to be gone, you want to learn that in October, not after you submit.

One more habit helps: write down where you found each detail. A line in a note is enough. It keeps you honest, and it makes the fact-check easy when you reread.

## Joining the two halves

The move is to connect one personal thread to one specific offering, and to say why they belong together. The personal thread should be something you have already shown in your application, or could show in a sentence. The offering should be something you found, not something you guessed.

Work a hypothetical. A student has spent three years volunteering with a county parks group, walking trails on weekends to log where the ground is washing out. She has never taken a statistics class, but she keeps wondering why the same stretches fail every spring. Suppose a mid-sized college (invented here, for illustration) offers an undergraduate course in environmental data analysis that works with a regional watershed agency's records.

The flat version:

*"I am passionate about the environment, and I am drawn to the college's strong environmental program and its commitment to sustainability."*

It could describe thousands of applicants and nearly any school. The specific version:

*"For three years I have logged trail erosion for a parks group, and I still cannot tell why the same slopes fail every spring. I want the tools to answer that. The college's environmental data course, which uses a watershed agency's real records, is where I would learn to do it."*

Here the personal half is a concrete habit with a real unanswered question. The school half is one named kind of offering, tied to that question. There is no list of perks, no praise of the faculty. The essay is saying: here is what I do, here is what I cannot yet do, here is where I would learn it.

A true story is enough. A modest interest, honestly described and carefully matched, beats a big claim that floats free of anything you have done.

## What to leave out

Some material tempts nearly everyone and almost never helps. Cut these first.

Rankings and prestige as the main reason. The college already knows where it ranks. Naming that as your reason tells it nothing about you, and it fails the swap test against every school of similar standing.

Generic praise of the campus or location. "Beautiful campus," "great city," "close-knit community." If a sentence could appear in a travel brochure, it does not belong here, unless the place connects to something you do. A student who loves night-sky photography and picked a school far from city lights has a reason. A student who likes pretty buildings does not.

Flattery. Telling a school it is "renowned" or "world-class" reads as filler. Specific interest is the compliment. In my experience, readers can tell the difference between a student who has done homework and a student who is trying to please.

A list of five things. Five weak links are worse than one strong one. When you name a lot of features, none of them connects to you, and the essay becomes a catalog. Pick the one offering that ties most directly to your thread, and give it room. If a second one truly belongs, mention it briefly. Resist the urge to prove you read the whole website.

Also check what each college is asking for. Prompts, word limits, and wording are set by each school and change from year to year, so read the current instructions for every application. Some schools do not ask the question at all, and some ask it in a different shape.

## When you cannot find a real reason

If you have done the research honestly and still cannot name a specific reason, that is useful information. It does not mean you are a bad writer. It may mean the school is on your list for the wrong reasons: a name you recognize, a parent's preference, a ranking you read once.

Be straight with yourself about it. Go back to your three wants. If the school offers none of them in any form you can point to, you are probably about to spend a lot of effort faking interest in a place that does not fit. Then ask whether it belongs on the list at all. Removing a school is a legitimate outcome of this exercise, and it saves you an application, a fee, and a weekend of forced prose.

Sometimes the problem is the other direction. You find a real reason, but it feels too small. Test it before you throw it out. A modest, true connection is usually enough, and the three tests above are a fair way to check.

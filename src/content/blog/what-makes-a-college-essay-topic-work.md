> **TL;DR**
> - A topic is an angle, not a subject. The thing that happened is the subject. What you make of it is the topic.
> - Test one: only you could have written it. Generic themes fail. A particular scene passes.
> - Test two: it is actually about you, not the person or event you are describing.
> - Test three: it leaves you room to reflect, so you can write another 250 to 300 words on what you made of it without repeating yourself.

Most students come to the essay with a subject already in mind. A job, a grandparent, a team, a move across the country. The subject is rarely the problem. The problem is that a subject is not yet a topic, and students spend weeks drafting before they notice the difference.

A workable topic passes three tests. Only you could have written it. It is actually about you. It leaves you room to reflect. This guide is about judging a topic against those three once you have one, and about fixing the angle when a topic almost works.

If you are still looking for a subject in the first place, [How to figure out what to write your college essay about](/blog/how-to-choose-college-essay-topic/) covers that ground. The [Common App personal statement](https://www.commonapp.org/apply/essay-prompts) is up to 650 words and goes to every Common App college on your list, so the topic is worth this much scrutiny.

## A topic is not a subject

Take a student who works a summer at her family's restaurant. That is the subject. It is a fine subject. It is also the same subject a few thousand other applicants will send in this year. The topic is the specific claim she makes about that summer. One student writes that the job taught her the value of hard work. Another writes that she spent June resenting customers who never looked up from their phones, and that by August she had started watching the ones who did, because she wanted to know what made them different. Same restaurant. Same hours. The second student has a topic. The first has a subject with a moral attached.

The check for whether you have a topic: can you state the angle in a sentence that someone else would not write? If the sentence could sit under anyone's name, you still have a subject.

## Test one: only you could have written it

This test is about specificity. Admissions readers move through a lot of essays in a sitting, and they remember the ones with a particular scene in them, not the ones with a general theme.

Before: I have always loved music. Playing piano taught me discipline and gave me an outlet for my emotions during hard times.

After: The metronome on our piano was broken, so my teacher counted out loud, and for six years I could not play a scale without hearing her voice going one-and-two-and. I still hear it. I hear it when I am nervous, which is not what discipline was supposed to feel like.

The first version is true for millions of people. The second could only come from one. The broken metronome is not decoration. It is the thing a reader carries out of the essay, and it is what makes the reflection that follows feel earned. When you test a topic, look for the detail that is yours alone. If there isn't one yet, the topic is not finished.

## Test two: it is actually about you

A common failure looks like a strong topic until you count the sentences. The essay is about a coach, a sick relative, a mentor, a historical figure the student admires. The other person gets three paragraphs of praise and the student gets two lines at the end about feeling inspired.

Run this check. Cross out every sentence that is about the other person and not about you. If less than half the essay is left, the topic is theirs, not yours. You can write about someone who matters to you, but the essay has to be about what their presence changed in you, said in specific terms.

The other version of this failure is picking a topic for how it sounds. MIT Admissions puts it plainly in [their guidance on the college essay](https://mitadmissions.org/blogs/entry/how-to-write-a-college-essay/): make the essay yours, and avoid buzzwords and banality. A topic chosen because it seems like the kind of thing admissions wants will read like exactly that. A topic you genuinely think about will not.

## Test three: it leaves you room to reflect

The best topics pair a small event with a large amount of thinking. Once you have described what happened, you should be able to write another 250 to 300 words on what you made of it, and those words should not repeat the description.

Try it with two versions of the same experience. A student reaches the final of a debate tournament and wins. Written as a topic, that is lidded. The story goes up to the trophy and stops, and the reflection is some version of hard work pays off. There is nowhere else to go.

Now the same student notices, somewhere in the quarterfinals, that she has argued three positions she does not hold and argued them well. That bothers her. What does it mean to be good at persuading people of things you are not sure are true? That is open. The event is small, a single afternoon, but the thinking runs well past 300 words and does not circle back on itself.

If you cannot see the second 250 words, the topic has a lid on it. Sometimes you can take the lid off by moving the angle, which the last section of this guide covers.

## A fourth thing that helps: you can already say something true about it out loud

This one is not a pass or fail test, but it predicts the other three well. If you cannot say the point of your essay plainly in conversation, the topic is not ready.

Try it on a parent or a friend. Not the whole essay, just the point. If it comes out as a paragraph of throat clearing, or if you hear yourself using words you would never normally say, the topic is still vague. If it comes out as a plain sentence that sounds like you, you have something to work with.

Rick Clark, who runs admissions at Georgia Tech, makes a related point about [reading the essay aloud](https://grownandflown.com/director-admission-college-essays/). Your ear catches what your eye skims. A sentence that is trying too hard sounds wrong when you say it, and a topic that is not really yours tends to sound wrong too.

## How the tests interact

A topic can clear one test and fail the next, which is why it helps to run all of them.

Consider a student who writes about rebuilding a motorcycle engine with his uncle. It passes test one easily. The detail is specific and nobody else has this exact story. It fails test two, because by the third paragraph the essay is a portrait of the uncle and the student is holding a wrench in the background.

Or a student who writes about the week her family spent without power after a storm. It passes test three. There is plenty to think about in that week, and most of it is about her parents and what they did not say out loud. It fails test one, because the way she has written it so far could be any family in that storm. The fix is not a new subject. It is one particular hour of that week, seen only by her.

## Pressure-testing a topic you already have

Run your current topic through these questions. Answer them out loud if you can.

- Could someone else at your school submit this same essay with a few names changed? If yes, the angle is too general.
- If you cross out every line that is about another person, is there half an essay left?
- Can you write 250 more words on what you made of it, without retelling the story?
- Can you say the point in one plain sentence that sounds like you talking?
- Is there one concrete detail in it that a reader would still remember an hour later?

A topic that gets a clean answer on four of five is in good shape. A topic that stumbles on two or more needs its angle reworked before you draft.

## If a topic fails a test

A failed test is usually a signal about the angle, not the subject. You can keep what happened and change what the essay is about.

College Essay Guy uses the sports injury to show this. As commonly written, the topic [fails when it is about the injury](https://www.collegeessayguy.com/blog/what-not-to-write-college-essay), the surgery and the long recovery. It succeeds when it is about the identity crisis that followed, the part where a student who had always been the athlete had to work out who they were without that. Same injury. Different topic.

The move is almost always in that direction, away from the event and toward what the event did to how you see yourself or the world. Johns Hopkins publishes a set of [essays that worked](https://apply.jhu.edu/college-planning-guide/essays-that-worked/) from admitted students, and the through line is not dramatic material. It is students taking ordinary, everyday life and finding the angle in it, a used car or a summer job or a set of family recipes. The subjects are small. The topics are not.

If saying the point out loud is the test, it helps to actually do it. That is the idea behind [Mascot](https://mascot-ai.com/app/talk-to-bear): you talk your experiences through, hear which ones have something behind them, and leave with a topic and notes. It does not write the essay.

Get the topic right and the draft gets easier. Get it wrong and no amount of line editing will save it.

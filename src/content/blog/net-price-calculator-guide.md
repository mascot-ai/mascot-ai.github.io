> - It takes about ten to fifteen minutes per school with your family's tax return and a few account balances in front of you.

The sticker price is the number on a college's website. The number that matters is the net price: what a school charges you after it subtracts the grants and scholarships you would receive.

You can find that number months before any school sends an aid offer. This guide walks through how, using a tool every eligible college is required to provide. If you are still building your list, start with [How to build a balanced college list](/blog/how-to-build-a-balanced-college-list/) and come back here once you have names to test.

## What is a net price calculator?

A net price calculator is a form on a college's website that asks about your family's finances and returns an estimate of what you would pay for one year.

The federal definition is simple. Net price is the cost of attendance minus the grant and scholarship aid you would get. Loans are not subtracted, because you have to repay them. The U.S. Department of Education's [National Center for Education Statistics](https://nces.ed.gov/ipeds/report-your-data/resource-center-net-price) describes the calculator as an estimate based on what students with similar profiles paid in a previous year.

The requirement comes from the Higher Education Opportunity Act of 2008 and took effect on October 29, 2011. It applies to every college that takes part in federal student aid programs and enrolls first-time, full-time undergraduates. The same NCES page says a student can use the tool without applying to the school.

Two things follow from that. First, you can run it on any school you are curious about, at no commitment. Second, because it reflects what past students received, it is a guide to a school's pattern of giving, not a promise to you.

## What goes into the cost of attendance?

Cost of attendance is the school's own estimate of one year's full expense. The Department of Education's [Federal Student Aid Handbook](https://fsapartners.ed.gov/knowledge-center/fsa-handbook/2026-2027/vol3/ch2-cost-attendance-budget) lists the standard pieces:

- Tuition and fees
- Food and housing
- Books, supplies and course materials
- Transportation
- Miscellaneous personal expenses

Schools set the allowances for the last four themselves, so two colleges in the same city can budget very different amounts for the same thing. When you compare calculators, look at which lines are inside the number. A school that budgets low for housing will look cheaper than it is.

## How do you run a net price calculator step by step?

Set aside fifteen minutes for each school and gather the documents first. The steps below work for almost every calculator.

### Step 1: Gather your inputs

You will usually need:

- Your parents' most recent tax return, or at least the adjusted gross income and taxes paid
- Untaxed income, such as child support received or retirement contributions
- Balances in checking, savings and investment accounts
- Household size and the number of people in college
- Your own income and savings, if any

Have a parent sit with you. Guessed inputs give you a bad number.

### Step 2: Find the right calculator

Search the school's name plus "net price calculator." Use the one on the college's own site. If the page is hard to find, try the admissions or financial aid section, since most schools put it there.

Forms differ from school to school. Some ask a handful of questions and some ask many more. Answer every optional question you can, since each one gives the school more to work with.

### Step 3: Enter your numbers and read the result

Fill in the form honestly. Do not round down to look needier, because the estimate only helps you if it matches your real situation.

When the result appears, look for three things: the cost of attendance, the grant and scholarship estimate, and the net price. A good calculator shows all three. Some also show a range.

### Step 4: Copy the result into one table

Open a spreadsheet and add one row per school. The table makes schools comparable at a glance:

| School | Sticker price | Estimated grants | Net price | Can we pay this? |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| | | | | |

Add a column for the date you ran it. A number from last spring may not match this fall's prices.

### Step 5: Run it again when anything changes

If a parent changes jobs, a sibling starts or finishes college, or you find out about an inheritance or a large payment, run the calculators again. A change in income or household size can move the estimate a lot.

## What does the estimate leave out?

The estimate is useful, but it has limits.

It is not an offer. A school can tell you in writing what it will give you only after you apply and are admitted. Most calculator pages say so in plain terms. Carnegie Mellon's [page](https://www.cmu.edu/admission/costs-aid/net-price-calculator), for example, describes its result as an estimate and not a guarantee.

It can be off for an individual family. A peer-reviewed study in the Journal of Student Financial Aid, ["In the Right Ballpark?"](https://ir.library.louisville.edu/jsfa/vol46/iss2/3), compared calculator estimates with actual aid at seven colleges. It found the estimates tracked actual aid well on average, but that individual results varied a great deal. The authors suggested that calculators show a range and let families add merit information. Treat your number as a ballpark.

It often leaves out merit aid. Many calculators estimate need-based grants and say little about scholarships tied to grades or test scores. If a school advertises automatic merit awards, check its scholarship page separately and subtract the amount you qualify for.

It reflects the school's own rules. Colleges handle things such as savings, home value and divorced parents in different ways. Read the notes at the top of each calculator so you know what it counted.

It cannot see the future. Tuition rises, and your family's situation can shift in the four years you would be enrolled. Ask each school how often it raises prices and whether aid changes after the first year.

## How do you compare net prices across schools?

Compare the net price column, not the sticker price. Then put one question next to every number: could we pay this for four years without borrowing more than we are comfortable with?

A [Government Accountability Office report](https://www.gao.gov/products/gao-23-104708) shows why you should do this before offers arrive. GAO estimated that 91 percent of colleges' aid offers left out or understated the net price. That is a good reason to have your own number ready. When an offer comes in, set it beside your calculator result and look for costs the offer did not include.

For a first look at the whole country, two federal tools help:

- The [College Scorecard](https://collegescorecard.ed.gov/) shows an average net price for each school.
- NCES's [College Navigator](https://nces.ed.gov/collegenavigator/) reports net price by family income band.

Both cover only first-time, full-time students who received federal aid, according to NCES's [average net price FAQ](https://nces.ed.gov/ipeds/report-your-data/faq-average-net-price), so they do not show what your family would pay. Use them to decide which schools deserve a calculator run. Then trust the school's own calculator over the average.

## What does this look like for a real student?

Here is an invented example with made-up numbers, to show the method.

Priya is a junior with a list of nine schools. Her parents have two children, and her older brother is in college. She runs the calculator at each school over two weekends and fills in her table.

Three schools come out well above what her family can pay, even though all three were targets for her. Two have a net price close to what a state school costs. One safety, a public university, comes out lower than anything else on the list, and its scholarship page shows an automatic award for her grade range that the calculator did not count.

Priya now has numbers instead of guesses. She moves the three expensive schools to an "only if aid improves" group, keeps the others, and adds a second affordable safety.

## What is a simple decision rule?

Use this on every school on your list:

> If the net price is more than your family can pay without taking on debt you are not willing to carry, treat the school as a reach financially, whatever its admission odds.

For a safety, hold a stricter line. A safety must be a school you would attend and can pay for. If it fails the second test, it is not a safety. [Reach, target, and safety: how to sort a school for your profile](/blog/reach-target-safety-schools/) explains how admission odds fit with this, and [Nine college list mistakes that lead to a rough April](/blog/college-list-mistakes/) covers what happens when families skip the cost check.

The fastest help is free: your school counselor can walk through a calculator with you, and most college financial aid offices will answer questions from prospective students. ## Frequently asked questions

### Do I need to be admitted before I can use a net price calculator?

No. NCES says prospective students can use the calculator without applying. Run it as early as you like.

### Is the net price the same as what I will actually pay?

Not exactly. Net price is the cost of attendance minus grants and scholarships. It does not subtract loans, so you may still have to find the money or borrow it. It is also an estimate, so your real offer can differ.

### What if a school's calculator is missing or broken?

Schools that qualify must post one, so contact the financial aid office and ask where it is. In the meantime, look at the school's page on the College Scorecard for an average net price, and treat it as a rough guide only.

### Should I use the average net price on College Navigator instead?

Use it to choose which schools to test, not to decide. The average covers students who received federal aid and does not describe an individual family, as NCES notes.

### Do I rerun the calculator every year?

Check the date on each result. Schools change their prices and aid, so rerun any calculator you used last year, and rerun it sooner if your finances change.

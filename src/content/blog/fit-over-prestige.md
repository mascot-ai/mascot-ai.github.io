> **TL;DR**
>
> - Where you go matters less than what you do there. The best-known research on this finds that students admitted to selective schools but who attended a less selective one earned about the same later.
> - There is one real exception: students from lower-income families seem to benefit more from selective schools. If that is you, weigh the extra aid and support, not the name.
> - Fit means five things you can check: academics, cost, support, environment, and what you can do there as a student.
> - Test your list with one question per school: in a bad semester, would this place still work for me?

Every fall, a student sits down with a counselor and says some version of the same thing: "I want to go to the best school I can get into." It sounds reasonable. It is also a sentence that often leads to a list nobody feels good about in April.

"Best" usually means "most famous" or "hardest to get into." Those are measures of how many people want a school, not of how well it will work for one student. This post makes the case for choosing on fit instead, shows what the evidence says, and gives you a way to check your own list. If you have not built a list yet, start with [How to build a balanced college list](/blog/how-to-build-a-balanced-college-list/) and come back.

## Does going to a more prestigious college change how your life turns out?

Less than most families assume. The most cited research on this question is a study by economists Stacy Dale and Alan Krueger. Comparing students who attended a more selective college with students who did not is misleading, because the first group was already different before they enrolled. So Dale and Krueger compared students who were accepted to similar schools and then chose different ones.

They found that, once you account for who the students were, attending the more selective school did not pay off in higher earnings. In their data, the average SAT score of the schools a student applied to predicted later income better than the average SAT score of the school the student actually attended. In plain terms, the kind of student who applies to ambitious schools tends to do well, wherever they land. The [NBER summary of the paper](https://www.nber.org/digest/dec99/payoff-attending-elite-college) is short and worth reading.

Two cautions. First, this is earnings research, and earnings are one measure of a good college experience. Second, other economists have used different methods and reached different conclusions, so treat this as strong evidence, not settled fact.

The exception matters too. The same study found that students from lower-income families did earn more after attending a selective college. If your family has little money to spare, a highly selective school with generous aid can still be a very good choice. The reason is the aid and the support around it, not the name on the diploma.

## What does matter more than the name?

What students do and who they meet once they are there. A large [Gallup and Purdue survey of more than 30,000 college graduates](https://news.gallup.com/opinion/gallup/173624/gallup-purdue-reveal-findings-college-graduates.aspx) found that the type of school mattered much less than the experiences students had. Graduates who had a professor who cared about them, who made them excited about learning, and who encouraged their goals had more than double the odds of being engaged at work and thriving in their well-being. Many graduates of top-ranked schools were no better off on those measures than graduates of other schools.

Colleges still differ a lot in class sizes, advising, how many students finish, and what they cost. Those are the differences worth checking, and none of them shows up in a ranking.

## How do you judge fit?

Fit is easy to talk about and hard to pin down, so here are five parts you can check on any school. Each one has a place to look.

1. **Academics.** Does the school have the major you think you want, and is it easy to change if you do not? Look at the course catalog, not the brochure. If the first-year schedule for your major is mostly large lectures, you should know that now.
2. **Cost.** What will your family actually pay? Run the school's net price calculator, which most colleges that take federal aid are required to post. The [College Scorecard](https://collegescorecard.ed.gov/) gives a first look at net price by income band.
3. **Support.** What happens when you struggle? Look at the share of first-year students who return for a second year, and at the graduation rate. Both are on [College Navigator](https://nces.ed.gov/collegenavigator/). A school that loses a quarter of its first-years is telling you something.
4. **Environment.** Size, location, and campus culture decide whether you will want to be there on a Tuesday in February. A visit, a virtual session, or a talk with a current student is worth more than any list of "best colleges for X."
5. **What you can do there.** Can a first-year student join a research group, get an internship, play in the ensemble, or start the club? At some schools those doors are open to everyone. At others they open mainly for upperclassmen or for students with a head start.

## What does this look like for a real student?

Here is an invented example, typical of what counselors see.

Maya is a junior with a strong transcript and an interest in marine biology. Her first list is all name recognition: four highly selective universities with famous names and one in-state flagship she added "just in case."

She runs the five checks on each school. At two of the selective schools, most marine biology work is done by graduate students, and first-years cannot get into a lab until sophomore year. A third has a strong program, but its net price is more than her family can pay and it offers no merit aid. The fourth passes on access and cost. The flagship has a marine science major, a first-year research program, a net price her family can manage, and a high first-year retention rate.

| School | Marine biology access | Net price for her family | Result |
|---|---|---|---|
| Selective A | Mostly grad-student work, labs from sophomore year | Manageable | Drop |
| Selective B | Mostly grad-student work, labs from sophomore year | Manageable | Drop |
| Selective C | Strong program | More than the family can pay | Drop |
| Selective D | Strong program, labs open to first-years | Manageable | Keep |
| In-state flagship | First-year research program | Manageable | Keep, move to the top |

After the checks, the flagship moves from "just in case" to the top of her list. She keeps Selective D, drops the other three, and adds two smaller schools with active marine labs. Her list is still ambitious. It is also a list where every school would work if it were her only acceptance. For how to sort that kind of list into reaches, targets, and safeties, see [Reach, target, and safety](/blog/reach-target-safety-schools/).

## What is a simple rule for choosing between schools?

Ask this about each one: if I had a bad semester, would this place still work for me? It pairs well with the safety check in [nine college list mistakes](/blog/college-list-mistakes/).

A bad semester means a hard class, a rough stretch at home, or a month when you are not sure about your major. A school that passes has advisors who will notice, a price your family can sustain for four years, and room to change direction. A school that fails may still be a good one. It is just a riskier bet than its name suggests.

If two schools tie, pick the one that is cheaper, then the one where you can name a specific thing you would do in your first semester.

## When does prestige actually help?

Sometimes. For some students a particular program is the only one that offers what they want, and some fields may recruit mainly from a short list of schools (check the recruiting pages of employers you care about). If that describes you, it is a fit reason, and you should say so on your list. The case to avoid is paying for a name when what you wanted is available at a school that fits you better and costs less.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is it a mistake to apply to Ivy League and other highly selective schools?

No. Apply if the schools pass your fit checks and the application will not crowd out the rest of your list. Aid varies a lot from one selective school to the next, so run each net price calculator before you rule one in or out. Just do not build the whole list around them, and make sure the list still has [real safeties](/blog/reach-target-safety-schools/).

### Do employers care where I went to college?

Some do, especially in a few industries and for a first job, and employers differ, so check the recruiting pages for the fields you want. What the research above does show is that how graduates fare at work tracks their experiences in college more than the type of school, and internships, projects, and mentors are where those experiences come from.

### How do I know a college is a good fit if I cannot visit?

Use virtual sessions, talk with current students through the admissions office, read the course catalog for your likely major, and check retention and graduation rates on College Navigator. Together these tell you more than a ranking does.

### What if my parents want me to go to the most prestigious school I get into?

Show them the five checks and the net price for each school. A conversation about specifics, such as what you would study, what it costs, and who will help you, usually goes better than an argument about names.

### Is fit just another word for "easier"?

No. A school can be demanding and still be a good fit. The point is that the challenge is the kind you want and that you have the support to meet it.

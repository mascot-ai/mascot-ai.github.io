> **TL;DR**
>
> - Most students do well with six to ten applications. The right number depends on how strong your list is, not on a rule.
> - Fewer than five leaves little room if a school says no. More than twelve usually means the list is not sorted yet.
> - Count schools by group: one or two reaches, three or four targets, two safeties you would be glad to attend.
> - Every extra application costs a fee and, for most schools, a set of supplemental essays. Stop adding when the essays start getting thinner.

Students ask this in the fall of senior year, usually after hearing that a friend applied to fifteen. The number follows from your list. Get the list right and the count mostly takes care of itself. For the full process, start with [How to build a balanced college list](/blog/how-to-build-a-balanced-college-list/).

## What is the usual number of applications?

Most students send somewhere between five and ten, and the average has been climbing. The Common App's end-of-season report for 2025 to 2026 says its first-year applicants [applied to an average of 7.06 institutions](https://www.commonapp.org/reports/end-season-report-2025-2026-first-year-application-trends), up from 6.79 the year before.

That average describes what students did, not what they should do. It mixes students who applied to one school with students who applied to twenty. Treat it as a rough midpoint. If your plan comes out at seven, you are in the ordinary range. If it comes out at three or at eighteen, look again at why.

## How many schools do you need in each group?

A balanced list has a few schools in each of three groups. If you are unsure how to sort them, read [Reach, target, and safety: how to sort a school for your profile](/blog/reach-target-safety-schools/) first.

A workable split for most students:

- One or two reaches, where admission is uncertain even for a strong applicant.
- Three or four targets, where your record looks like the students the school usually admits.
- Two safeties, where you are very likely to be admitted, your family can afford it, and you would be glad to go.

That is six to eight schools. Add one only if it fills a real gap, such as a second option in a region you care about or a program your other schools lack.

## Why not apply to more schools to improve your odds?

More applications do raise the chance of at least one yes, but the gain shrinks fast and the cost does not.

Each application takes time. Most colleges ask for supplemental essays on top of the main essay, and a "why us" essay only works when you know the school well. The twelfth application usually gets a worse essay than the third did, and a weak essay at a reach school can cost you the odds the extra application was meant to buy.

There is a money cost too. Application fees add up across a long list. If your family may qualify for a fee waiver, ask your school counselor how it works at the colleges you have in mind.

Adding reaches does not make a list safer either. Ten schools that each admit one applicant in ten is still a hard list. Only the safeties make it safer.

## Why not apply to fewer?

A very short list is fragile. Apply to three schools, hear no from the two you care about most, and you are left with one option you may not love. Admission to a given school is hard to predict, even for strong students.

Five is a reasonable floor for most students. Go lower only if you are applying through a binding early program with a clear backup, or to a state system that admits by formula, and you have talked it through with a counselor.

## What does this look like for a real student?

Take a made-up student, Priya. She has a 3.8 unweighted GPA, wants to study biology, and her family can pay about $20,000 a year after aid. The schools below are invented for the example.

| Group | Schools | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Reach | 2 | One very selective private, one selective public in another state |
| Target | 3 | Two mid-sized public universities, one small private with a strong biology program |
| Safety | 2 | An in-state public with a net price near $12,000 and an honors program she likes, and a second school with automatic merit aid |

That makes seven. Priya checks it and finds that both safeties have strong biology departments, and she can name a reason for each target. Then a friend suggests two more reaches. The rule below tells her not to add them.

## What is a simple rule for deciding how many?

Work down this order and stop when you have an answer.

1. Fill the safety slots first. You need two schools you would be glad to attend and can afford. If you cannot find two, finding them is the work, not adding reaches.
2. Add targets until you have three or four you can describe in a sentence ("strong biology, mid-sized, near home").
3. Add one or two reaches you would still be proud to attend if admitted.
4. Count your supplemental essays. If you cannot write each one with real detail about that school before your earliest deadline, cut the school you know least.

If you finish step 4 at six to ten, you are done. If you are above ten, look for repeats: two targets with the same selling point, or a third reach added out of habit.

## How should early deadlines change the count?

Early rounds change the order of your work, not the size of your list. If you plan to apply early to one school, your regular-round list still needs its full set of safeties and targets, because an early application can come back as a no or a deferral. Build the whole list before the first deadline so you are not choosing safeties in a hurry after a bad December.

Check each school's own deadlines and whether its early option is binding. The school's admissions page is the source, not a forum thread.

## Checklist: is your number right?

- I have at least two schools I could afford and would be glad to attend.
- Every school on the list has a reason I can state in a sentence.
- I can write a real supplemental essay for each school before its deadline.
- I checked the net price at each school with its own calculator.
- No two schools are on the list for the same reason.

A "no" on any line points to the school to fix or cut.

## What should you do if your list is already too long?

Rank the schools by how much you would want to attend, not by prestige. Then cut from the bottom of each group, starting with any school whose essays you dread. Eight applications you can finish well beat twelve you rush.

> **TL;DR**
>
> - Junior year is for a long, loose list of 12 to 20 schools. Senior fall is for cutting it to a final list.
> - Do it in five passes: say what you want, collect schools, check the numbers, check the price, then cut.
> - Aim to finish junior year with a working list of 8 to 12 schools and at least two safeties you can afford.
> - Nothing on the list is final until the summer after. Your grades and your taste will both shift.

Most students start their list in the fall of senior year, with applications due in weeks. A junior who starts earlier has time to visit a campus, run the money numbers, and change their mind before any deadline is close. You don't have to finish early, but you should look at enough schools that the final choices feel like yours.

This post is the junior-year plan. For the full method of sorting a list, read [How to build a balanced college list](/blog/how-to-build-a-balanced-college-list/).

## Why build the list in junior year?

The slow parts of a list are not the schools. They are the things that take a calendar: a campus visit, a talk with your parents about money, a spring semester that changes your grades, a summer that changes what you want to study.

A junior also has more room to explore. You are not under deadline pressure yet, so you can look at schools you would never have time for in October of senior year.

## What do you need before you start?

Not much.

- Your transcript so far, with your GPA and the rigor of your classes.
- A rough sense of your family's budget. A single number is fine, even if it is "we need to talk."
- An hour a week. That is enough for the first four passes.
- Your school counselor, whom you should meet at least once this year. They know which colleges your school sends students to and what those students tend to get.

## Pass 1: What do you want from a college?

**By the end of this pass, you will have a one-page description of your ideal school, in plain language.**

Before you look at a single school, write down what you care about. Use sentences, not a ranking. Start with these questions:

1. What would you like to study, and what if you change your mind?
2. Do you want a large school, a small one, or do you not know yet?
3. Do you want to be near home, a day's travel from home, or far away?
4. What kind of place do you want to spend four years in: a city, a college town, a campus out of the way?
5. What would make you transfer? What would make you stay?

Keep the answers loose. "I want to try a few subjects before I pick one" is a useful answer, because it rules out schools that make you declare a major on day one.

**Check:** a friend could read the page and guess what kind of school you might like.

## Pass 2: Which schools go on the long list?

**By the end of this pass, you will have 15 to 20 schools, with no judging yet.**

Collect first and judge later. Good places to look:

- Schools your school counselor says have admitted students like you.
- A free search tool such as the [College Scorecard](https://collegescorecard.ed.gov/), which lets you filter by size, location, and degrees offered.
- Schools your older siblings, cousins, or friends' older siblings liked or hated, and why.
- A few schools that surprise you. Pick one or two you have never heard of that match your page from Pass 1.

Write each school on one line with a one-sentence reason it made the list. If you cannot give a reason, take it off.

A ranking tells you what a publisher measured, not whether you would be happy there. Use it to find schools you missed, then judge each one by your own page.

## Pass 3: Where do you stand at each school?

**By the end of this pass, each school on your list will have a rough label: reach, target, or safety.**

Start with the school's [Common Data Set](https://commondataset.org/), a standard set of data definitions that colleges use to report the same facts the same way. Most colleges publish one, and you can find it by searching the school's name plus "Common Data Set." Section C covers first-year admissions: how many applied, how many were admitted, and the test score and GPA ranges of the students who enrolled. Two cautions:

- Check whether the GPA is weighted or unweighted before you compare it to yours.
- Test numbers describe students who submitted scores. At a test-optional school, that is not everyone who enrolled.

Then compare your own record with the middle of those ranges. If you are well above them, it is probably a safety. If you are inside them, a target. If you are below them or the school admits a small share of applicants, a reach. The full process, with a worked example, is in [Reach, target, and safety: how to sort a school for your profile](/blog/reach-target-safety-schools/).

You are a junior, so these are estimates. Your grades in the spring and the first half of senior year will move some schools from one group to another. Write the labels in pencil.

**Check:** you have at least a few schools in each group, and nothing is labeled safety only because you like it.

## Pass 4: Can your family afford each school?

**By the end of this pass, you will have a net price estimate for every school you are serious about.**

The sticker price is not what most families pay. Net price is the cost after grants and scholarships. Every college that takes part in federal student aid and enrolls first-time, full-time undergraduates must post a net price calculator on its website, as the Department of Education's [guidance on the requirement](https://fsapartners.ed.gov/knowledge-center/library/dear-colleague-letters/2013-02-27/gen-13-07-subject-guidance-implementing-net-price-calculator-requirement) explains.

For each school:

1. Search the school's name plus "net price calculator."
2. Fill it in with your family, using real numbers. Ask a parent to sit with you.
3. Write down the estimate and the date.

The Scorecard also shows an average net price for each school, which works as a quick first check. The calculator on the school's own site is more personal, so trust it over the average.

Two things to know. The estimates are only estimates, since a final aid offer arrives after you are admitted. And compare net prices, not sticker prices. The two can differ a lot, so a school that looks expensive on paper may not be once aid is counted.

**Check:** at least two schools that you would be glad to attend are ones your family can afford. If not, finding them is the main job for the spring, ahead of adding anything else.

## Pass 5: Which schools do you cut?

**By the end of this pass, you will have a working list of 8 to 12 schools.**

Spring of junior year is the time to cut, because by then you have your Pass 1 page, your labels, and your net prices. Look at each school and ask three things:

1. Can I say in one sentence why I want to go there?
2. Can my family afford it, or do I have a real plan if not?
3. Is it different from the other schools on the list, or is it the same school twice?

Cut any school where you cannot give a one-sentence reason, and any your family cannot afford with no plan to close the gap. If two schools are on the list for the same reason, keep the one you like more.

Then count. A working list of 8 to 12 gives you room to lose a few schools over the summer and still end up with a final list of 6 to 10. If you want the reasoning behind that range, read [How many colleges should you actually apply to?](/blog/how-many-colleges-should-you-apply-to/)

## What should you do in each season?

The five passes fit into the year like this. Dates are loose and your school's calendar will shift them.

| When | What to do |
|---|---|
| Fall of junior year | Pass 1. Meet your school counselor. Start collecting schools for Pass 2. |
| Winter | Finish Pass 2. Start Pass 3 on your top ten or so. |
| Spring | Finish Passes 3 and 4. Cut to a working list in Pass 5. Visit a school or two if you can, even a local one just to learn what a campus feels like. |
| Summer | Revise the list. Read the application requirements for each school. Start thinking about essays. |

The Common App [opened for the 2026-27 season on August 1](https://www.commonapp.org/blog/common-app-opens-application-launch-2026-27-season/), and it has opened around the start of August in recent years. Each college sets its own deadlines, and early rounds fall in the fall of senior year, so check each school's admissions page for the dates that apply to you. A working list in place by August means you spend the summer on essays instead of on the list.

## What does this look like for a real student?

Take a made-up junior, Marcus. He likes engineering and music, wants a mid-sized school within a day's drive, and his family can pay about $15,000 a year after aid. The schools are invented.

In the fall he writes his page: "mid-sized, engineering with a real music scene, not a huge city, within a day's drive of home." He picks up 17 schools by January from his counselor, the Scorecard, and his cousin.

By March he has sorted them. Three are reaches, six are targets, four are safeties, and four more he cannot label because their data is thin. He runs net price calculators for the ten he likes best and finds that two targets come out well above $15,000, while one safety, a public university with an honors program, comes out near $11,000.

In April he cuts. Two of the reaches go, because he only likes them for their names. He drops the two expensive targets with no plan to cover the gap, and a visit in April shows him that one school he could not label is a target. He ends the spring with ten schools: one reach, five targets, four safeties. In August he adds one more reach after visiting over the summer.

That is a working list, and it is Marcus's. It came out of a page he wrote himself.

## What mistakes should juniors avoid?

- A list of famous schools tells you nothing about fit. Start from your Pass 1 page.
- Waiting until April of senior year to look at money is too late. Run the calculators in junior year, because a school you can't afford is a reach however likely admission is.
- The list will change, and that is why you build it early. Don't treat it as final.
- If you don't like your safeties, find better ones now, while there is time.
- Your parents and friends can help, but the page in Pass 1 has to be yours.

## Checklist: is your junior-year list on track?

- I wrote what I want from a college in my own words.
- I have 12 to 20 schools with one reason each.
- I looked up Common Data Set numbers for the schools I am serious about.
- I ran the net price calculator at my top ten.
- I have at least two safeties I would be glad to attend and can afford.
- I met with my school counselor.
- My working list has 8 to 12 schools by the end of spring.

## What should you do next?

Start with Pass 1 this week. It takes about twenty minutes and it makes every later step easier. Then put the other passes on your calendar, one a month.

> **TL;DR**
> - Ordinary moments often outperform dramatic ones as essay topics, because the drama does the work that your reflection should be doing.
> - The real risk with a small moment is thinness, not triviality. A moment can be too small to hold an essay, but it is never too small because it lacks a headline.
> - Look for a specific moment, one you keep returning to or one that happened once and stuck, not a big life event.
> - Before you commit, run it through the "so what" test: can you say what it changed in you, and do you have room to keep reflecting past the description.

A lot of students walk into essay season assuming the topic has to be big. A championship, a diagnosis, a move across an ocean, something with enough weight that a reader will sit up. If nothing like that has happened to you, the assumption goes, you don't have a topic yet.

That's backwards. The size of the event and the strength of the essay barely correlate, and the moments that read as small on paper often carry more thinking than the ones that read as dramatic. The question is not whether your moment is impressive. It's whether there's enough underneath it.

## Why small moments often work better

A small moment starts you two-thirds of the way to a working topic, and it does that without any effort on your part.

[How to figure out what to write your college essay about](/blog/how-to-choose-college-essay-topic/) lays out three things a topic needs: it has to be specific, it has to be genuinely yours, and it has to leave you room to reflect. A dramatic event tends to fail the first two by default. The championship game, the medical scare, the big move: thousands of other applicants have some version of the same story, and the drama itself tends to crowd out anything particular to you. You end up describing what happened to you rather than what you noticed.

A small moment doesn't have that problem. If you write about the specific way your grandfather folded a napkin every night at dinner, nobody else has that detail. It's already narrow, and it's already yours by default, because nobody else was standing where you were standing. Johns Hopkins publishes a collection of ["Essays That Worked"](https://apply.jhu.edu/college-planning-guide/essays-that-worked/) from admitted students each year, and the material in them is not dramatic. It's daily life: a car, a recipe, a habit, a job. The pattern holds across a lot of admitted essays, which suggests the size of the subject was never the thing doing the work.

MIT Admissions makes the same point from the other direction. In ["On Essay Writing,"](https://mitadmissions.org/blogs/entry/on-essay-writing/) Krystal L. '17 writes that not everyone has been student body president or led a team to a title, and that's fine, because the same qualities a big achievement would show up in a small, understated moment too. You don't need the headline version of a trait. You need a moment where the trait actually showed up, even if nobody else was watching.

So a small moment gets you specificity and ownership almost automatically. What it does not hand you for free is the third test: whether there's enough there to think about. That's the part worth slowing down on.

## What makes an ordinary moment usable

The risk with a small moment isn't that it's too plain. It's that it might have nothing underneath it.

Plainness is fine. A moment where you refilled a ketchup bottle, waited for a bus, or lost an argument with your younger sibling can all carry an essay. What can't carry an essay is a moment where the description is the whole thing, where once you've said what happened, there is nothing left to say about what it meant. That's a different failure than being too ordinary, and it's the one to actually watch for.

[What makes a college essay topic actually work](/blog/what-makes-a-college-essay-topic-work/) covers the full three-test framework, and the third test is the one that does the most work on a small moment specifically: after you've described what happened, can you write another 250 to 300 words about what you made of it, without repeating the description. A championship win often fails this test because the story ends at the trophy. A quiet, ordinary moment can pass it easily, because there was never a plot to finish, just a thing you keep turning over.

The way to check is to try to answer "so what" honestly. Say what happened in two or three sentences, then ask what it changed in how you think, what you noticed about yourself, or what question it left you with. If a real answer comes, even a small or unresolved one, you likely have a topic. If the honest answer is "nothing, it was just a normal Tuesday," the moment isn't ready yet, and it might not be one you can force.

## Where to look

Small moments tend to hide in the same handful of places. A few worth checking:

- A routine or commute you've done enough times to stop noticing, until one day you didn't
- A recurring interaction with the same person, a regular at a job, a neighbor, a bus driver
- A small disagreement, the kind that didn't get resolved cleanly and still bothers you a little
- A chore or job task you've done so many times it became automatic
- A collection or habit you keep for reasons you've never had to explain out loud
- A mistake you made and noticed, even a minor one, that changed how you did something afterward

None of these needs to be dressed up. The moment doesn't need a twist. It needs a specific instance you can describe in a few sentences, and a genuine question or shift sitting underneath it.

## Trying it on one moment: a regular at work

Take the second item on that list: a recurring interaction with the same person. Say you work weekend shifts at a coffee shop, and there's a customer who comes in every Saturday morning, orders the same thing, and never says more than that order out loud.

The subject is the regular customer. That's not yet a topic. A flat, before version of this essay describes him: what he orders, what he wears, how long he's been coming in, maybe a line about how "everyone has a story." That version is description with a moral stapled to the end, and there's nothing underneath it that a reader hasn't already guessed.

The after version finds the angle by noticing something specific and sitting with it. Maybe you started guessing what kind of week he'd had by how he said his order, fast and clipped on bad weeks, slower on good ones, and you never once heard him complete a full sentence to you in eight months. What does it mean that you built an entire read on a stranger out of four words a week? What did that make you notice about how much information you actually need from someone to feel like you know them? Where else in your life have you been doing the same shortcut without realizing it? That's a topic. The customer is barely in it by the second half. You are.

The moment itself never changed. What changed is which layer the essay is written from: the surface event, or the thinking that event kept generating in you over time.

## When small really is too small

Some ordinary moments really are just ordinary, and it's worth saying that plainly instead of pretending every moment has a hidden essay in it if you dig long enough.

The honest signal is this: after real effort, describe the moment in a few sentences, then try to write 250 more words about what it meant, without those words just being more description dressed up as reflection. If you can do that and the second 250 words feel true and new, the moment is carrying its weight. If you keep circling back to restating what happened, or reaching for a lesson that sounds like it belongs to someone else, the moment probably doesn't have the second layer yet, and pushing harder on the same memory usually doesn't produce it. That's not a failure. It just means you look at a different moment, not that you need a bigger one.

Small moments are easy to talk past and hard to write past. Saying one out loud, and having someone ask what it actually meant, is often what surfaces the part worth writing. That is what a conversation with [Mascot](https://mascot-ai.com/app/talk-to-bear) does: you talk through the moment, and it helps you find what's underneath it. It does not write the essay.

The ordinary moment was never the weak choice. It's usually the one with the most room to think in.

> **TL;DR**
>
> - Using AI to think out loud and get feedback on your own draft is different from having it write sentences you submit as yours.
> - Common App treats presenting AI output as your own work as fraud. Most colleges have no written rule, and 30% of those surveyed ban AI for writing the essay.
> - A quick test: did the words come from you first, and could you defend every sentence out loud?
> - Check each school's own instructions. They differ.

Plenty of seniors wonder about this and are too nervous to ask their counselor. So here is a straight answer: some uses of AI on a college essay are fine, and some are not. The line is real, even if nobody has drawn it in ink.

## What do the rules actually say?

The Common App [fraud policy](https://www.commonapp.org/fraud-policy/) lists as fraud intentionally misrepresenting as your own original work "the substantive content or output of an artificial intelligence platform, technology, or algorithm." Applicants also affirm that what they submit is their own work, and false information can lead to disciplinary action, including revoked admission.

The word "intentionally" matters. The policy does not say you may never touch an AI tool. It says you can't hand in its output and call it yours. Where "substantive" begins isn't defined, and that gap is what makes students nervous.

Colleges are no clearer. In a [Kaplan survey](https://kaplan.com/about/press-media/college-admissions-officers-survey-2025-essays-ai) of more than 200 admissions officers (released November 2025), 30% said their college bans generative AI for writing the essay, 2% allow it, and 68% have no policy. For brainstorming, 27% allow it and 4% ban it. For feedback on a draft you wrote yourself, 21% allow it and 5% ban it. The rest have said nothing.

The further a use is from "the AI wrote it," the more schools tolerate it. But "no policy" doesn't mean "approved."

## Where is the line, in plain terms?

Mine is about who produced the words and the ideas first.

Generally fine:

- Talking through your experiences to figure out what to write about.
- Asking for questions that help you remember a specific moment.
- Getting feedback on a draft you wrote: where it drags, where it confuses, where it sounds like someone else.
- Checking spelling and grammar.

Not fine:

- Asking an AI to write the essay, a paragraph, or an opening line, then using it.
- Pasting in your resume and asking for "a story."
- "Polishing" a draft until little of your own wording is left.
- Inventing or stretching an experience because the AI suggested it.

Gray, so read the school's instructions or ask:

- Translating an essay you wrote in another language.
- Having an AI rewrite a single clumsy sentence.

In the gray zone, take the stricter reading. Rewriting that one sentence yourself takes five minutes. A fraud finding on your application doesn't go away in five minutes.

## Why is "let it write a first draft" a trap?

Even if you never plan to submit AI text, a draft from a model changes what you write. It hands you a structure, a tone, and a lesson at the end, and you edit toward it instead of away from it.

You end up with a tidy arc, a vague moral, and no particular person in it. A strong essay has a moment only you could have had, and an AI doesn't have your moment. It has a blend of everyone else's.

So apart from the rules, the practical case is the same. The essay is the one place in your application where the reader hears you. Hand it over and it has no reason to exist. For what makes a topic work in the first place, see [What makes a college essay topic actually work](/blog/what-makes-a-college-essay-topic-work/).

## Can colleges tell?

Sometimes, but it's the wrong thing to build your plan around. You don't need software to notice an essay that is smooth, generic, and out of step with the rest of your file. If your activities list says you love building things and your essay talks about "the tapestry of human experience," someone will notice.

Ask yourself this instead: if an admissions officer asked you about the essay, could you explain why you chose each detail? If so, you're fine. If you'd shift in your seat, that tells you something.

## A quick test before you submit

- I can point to the moment the essay is about, and it really happened.
- The first draft came from me, sentence by sentence.
- If a tool helped, it asked me questions or pointed out problems. It did not supply wording or ideas.
- I read the school's own instructions on AI and followed them.
- I could talk about any sentence for a minute without notes.

If you can't tick all five, change the essay, not the checklist.

## What if you're stuck?

Most students reach for AI because they don't know what to write, not because they want to cheat. That problem has a fix that doesn't involve generated text. Talking through your own experiences out loud is often enough to find the moment that matters.

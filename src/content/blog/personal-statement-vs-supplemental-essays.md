> **TL;DR**
> - The personal statement and supplemental essays are not the same assignment. Do not treat them like one.
> - The personal statement is about who you are, broadly. Supplements are about fit with one specific school or program.
> - Don't force your personal-statement topic into a supplement just because it worked once. It usually answers the wrong question there.
> - Don't try to write one flexible supplement that could go to any school either. That answers no question well.
> - Pick a topic for each essay based on the job that essay is doing, not on what you already have written.

Two mistakes show up over and over in application season. The first is reusing the same essay, or a lightly reworded version of it, across every prompt a student faces. The second is the opposite problem: treating every single essay, including two-hundred-word supplements, as another wide-open personal statement, and trying to cram a whole life story into a box that was never built for one.

Both backfire for the same reason. Each essay in an application is doing a specific job for the reader on the other end. The personal statement and a "why this school" supplement are not two versions of the same task. They are two different tasks that happen to both be called "essays." Once you know what job each one is doing, choosing a topic for it gets a lot more straightforward.

## What the personal statement is actually for

This cluster has covered the personal statement in detail elsewhere, so the short version here: it's the one essay that goes to every college on your Common App list, up to [650 words](https://www.commonapp.org/apply/essay-prompts), and its job is to tell an admissions reader who you are in general. It is not tied to any one school. If you want the fuller process for finding that topic, [see the pillar post on choosing a college essay topic](/blog/how-to-choose-college-essay-topic/).

Because the personal statement travels to every school unchanged, it can't be about why you want to attend a specific program or campus. It has to work as a piece of writing that introduces you as a person, on its own, no matter which college opens the file.

## What a supplemental essay is actually for

A supplemental essay is set by the individual college, not by the Common App. Each school writes its own prompts, sets its own word limits, and can change both from year to year. Word limits vary school to school, and they are usually a good deal shorter than the personal statement's 650 words. You have to check the actual requirements for each college you're applying to; there's no single number that applies across the board.

The narrower length comes with a narrower question. A supplement usually isn't asking "who are you." It's asking something closer to "why you, and why here, specifically." The reader already has your personal statement in the file. The supplement's job is to test whether you actually know the school you're applying to, or whether you could have sent this same paragraph to ten other colleges without changing a word.

## The five supplement types, and what each one is really asking

Most supplements fall into a handful of recognizable categories. Knowing which type you're looking at changes what a strong topic looks like.

### Why this school

This prompt asks you to connect something specific about the college to something specific about you. A weak answer lists things anyone could find on the homepage: campus size, a well-known ranking, a general reputation for a strong program. A strong answer names a particular class, research lab, advising structure, or campus tradition and ties it to something you actually want to do there. The common mistake is writing an answer that could be mailed to any school on your list just by swapping the name.

### Why this major

This one narrows the "why here" question to a field of study. It's asking how you got interested in the subject and what you'd do with it at this particular school, using their specific curriculum or opportunities as the anchor. The mistake to avoid is describing your interest in the subject in general terms without ever landing on why this college's version of that major matters to you.

### Community or diversity essay

This prompt asks what you'd bring to a campus community, not just what the campus would give you. It's looking for something concrete: a perspective, a background, a way you've contributed to a group before, and some sense of how that would show up on this campus. The mistake is staying abstract, writing about diversity as a concept instead of writing about your own specific experience of it.

### Activity or extracurricular expansion

This one asks you to go deeper on something your activities list only summarized in a line or two. A resume entry says you ran a robotics build team. This essay is where you explain what that actually involved, what went wrong, what you decided, what changed because of it. The mistake is repeating the resume line in sentence form instead of adding detail the reader couldn't already see.

### Short answer or quick-list questions

These are the smallest prompts, sometimes just a sentence or two, sometimes a list format. They're testing whether you can be specific under real constraint. A strong short answer picks one concrete detail instead of trying to summarize an entire interest in fifteen words. The mistake is writing something so general it could answer three different quick-list prompts at once.

## Can you reuse a topic across essays

Sometimes. What you usually can't reuse is the same topic told the exact same way, because the job of each essay is different.

Say a student spends weekends restoring an old radio, learning circuits and soldering from online forums because no one at home could teach her. In a personal statement, that topic might be about patience, about teaching yourself something with no instructor and no shortcut, and what that says about how she works. In a "why this major" supplement for a college's electrical engineering program, the same underlying interest shows up differently: now it's about the specific lab equipment or first-year project sequence at that school that would let her go further with circuits than a bedroom workbench ever could. Same thread, two different jobs, two different pieces of writing.

Whichever essay you're writing, the topic still has to hold up on its own terms. [The three tests for a college essay topic](/blog/what-makes-a-college-essay-topic-work/) apply to a 150-word supplement just as much as they apply to a 650-word personal statement. A topic that's actually yours, that has enough underneath it to say something real, and that answers the actual question asked: that bar doesn't move just because the word count shrinks.

## How to find supplement topics without starting over

You don't need eight separate topic searches for eight supplements. If you've already brainstormed for your personal statement, you likely have more raw material sitting around than you think.

Go back through [the list of questions for finding your essay story](/blog/questions-to-find-your-college-essay-story/) and look at what you already wrote down, not for a new idea, but for which existing idea actually answers a specific school's specific question. A moment you jotted down about a summer research project might be a weak fit for your personal statement but a strong answer to one college's "why this major" prompt. A detail about organizing a school event might not carry a whole personal statement, but it might be exactly what a community essay is asking for. The search isn't always for something new. Often it's for the right match between what you already have and the question a given school is actually asking.

Sorting out which topic belongs where gets easier once you can say out loud what each essay is actually asking. That is part of what a conversation with [Mascot](https://mascot-ai.com/app/talk-to-bear) does: you talk through your topics, and it helps you see which one answers which question. It does not write the essay.

Different essays, different jobs. Pick the topic to match the job, and the writing gets a lot less confusing from there.

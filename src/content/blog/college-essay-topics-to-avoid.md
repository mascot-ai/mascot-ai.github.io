> **TL;DR**
> - No college essay topic is officially banned. Not the sports injury, not the immigrant-grandparent story, none of them.
> - Certain subjects recur every single cycle. An admissions reader has likely seen a version of yours already this week.
> - The fix is almost never the subject itself. It is the angle you take on it. See [what makes a college essay topic actually work](/blog/what-makes-a-college-essay-topic-work/) for the three tests to run on any idea.
> - A few topics are worth pruning anyway, but for reasons that have nothing to do with being "overdone."

You have probably read one of those lists. "10 college essay topics to never write about." You scroll it, find your idea on there (the injury, the trip, the grandparent), and your stomach drops a little. Weeks of thinking, gone, because some blog told you the topic is dead on arrival.

The honest version: admissions officers really do see the same handful of subjects come up constantly. That part of the warning is true. But "common" and "disqualifying" are not the same thing, and most of the advice online blurs them together until a perfectly good essay idea gets thrown out for no reason. [How to figure out what to write your college essay about](/blog/how-to-choose-college-essay-topic/) walks through how to land on an idea in the first place. This post is about what to do when the idea you already have is one of the "common" ones, and whether that actually matters.

### The sports injury or the big game

This one goes generic the moment the essay spends its word count narrating the play-by-play: the score, the injury, the recovery timeline, like a highlight reel instead of a reflection. It works when the injury is really the setup for a different story, the one about who you were before you could rely on your body and who you had to become when you couldn't.

### The service trip or mission trip

The generic version reads like a travel journal: what the group built, how grateful the local kids were, how "changed" the writer felt by day three. A version worth keeping usually zooms in on one specific, uncomfortable moment (a conversation, a mistake, a thing you assumed and were wrong about) rather than the whole itinerary.

### The immigrant-family or grandparent's-sacrifice story

This goes flat when the essay is really a tribute to the relative and the writer barely appears in their own paper. The angle that saves it turns the lens back around: what did you inherit from that sacrifice that shows up in how you actually make decisions now, not just how proud you are of them.

### The death of a relative

The risk here is writing grief as a general, familiar experience: everyone feels sad, everyone misses someone, and the essay could have been written by almost anyone who has lost someone. It holds up when it gets specific about how that loss changed one particular habit, relationship, or way you see something ordinary, not the loss itself in the abstract.

### Moving to a new place

The flat version is a chronology: we moved, it was hard, then I adjusted. Nothing here that only happened to you. It works when the essay is really about a specific skill the move forced you to build, like reading a new social room fast, and where else that skill has shown up since.

### "My hero is my parent"

This one drifts into a list of a parent's admirable qualities, essentially a tribute speech, and again the writer disappears from their own essay. The rescue is the same move as the grandparent story: show one specific thing you do differently, or one decision you made, because of watching them do it, not just the fact that you admire them.

### The resume, restated in essay form

College Essay Guy calls this one out specifically: cramming a long list of accomplishments, clubs, and awards into 650 words feels exactly as cramped as it sounds, because a [Common App personal statement](https://www.commonapp.org/apply/essay-prompts) tops out at 650 words and a list needs way more room than that to land. It works, if it works at all, only when you drop the list and go deep on one single thing from it, told as a real scene instead of a bullet point.

## Why "overdone" is mostly a myth

Rick Clark, who runs undergraduate admissions at Georgia Tech, put this plainly in a [2022 piece for Grown and Flown](https://grownandflown.com/director-admission-college-essays/): "there is no utterly unique topic," and "knowing the topic won't differentiate you." That is not a loophole. It is the actual rule. Readers are not sorting essays into a pile by subject and rejecting the crowded ones. They are reading for something specific to the writer inside whatever subject shows up.

Try this test on your own draft. Could a hundred other applicants who lived through roughly the same thing (the same injury, the same move, the same kind of loss) have written your exact essay, sentence for sentence? If the honest answer is yes, the subject isn't the problem, the angle is. That is exactly what [what makes a college essay topic actually work](/blog/what-makes-a-college-essay-topic-work/) covers in more detail: specific, actually about you, and room to reflect.

## The sports-injury case, worked through

College Essay Guy's framing on this one is useful because it draws a clean line: a sports essay fails when it's about the injury, and succeeds when it's about the identity crisis that followed. That line matters more than which sport, which injury, or how serious it was.

Picture two drafts from the same event: a torn ACL during a junior-year season. Draft one spends four paragraphs on the tear, the surgery, the crutches, the slow return to the field. It ends with "I learned resilience." Draft two skips most of that and instead sits with the six weeks the writer couldn't practice and, for the first time since age seven, didn't know who they were without the sport attached to their name. Same injury. Completely different essay, because the second one is about a question, not an event.

## When a topic really should go

None of this means every topic survives a rewrite. Two situations are worth being honest about. If something happened recently enough that you genuinely cannot get distance from it yet, an essay about it will likely stay reactive instead of reflective, and that is a timing problem rather than a subject problem. And if a topic is really about someone else (a parent, a sibling, a friend) with you mostly narrating from the sidelines, no angle fixes that until you find the part of the story where you are actually the one making a choice. Neither of these is about the topic being "overdone." They are about whether you can currently write your way into the reflection the topic needs.

Working out which angle actually rescues a topic is easier said out loud than worked out alone on a page. That is what a conversation with [Mascot](https://mascot-ai.com/app/talk-to-bear) is for: you talk through the subject, and it helps you find the angle that is actually yours. It does not write the essay.

If your topic is on this list, it's probably fine. Check the angle before you scrap the idea.

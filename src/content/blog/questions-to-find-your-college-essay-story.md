> **TL;DR**
> - This is a fast brainstorm, not a worksheet. Don't overthink any single question.
> - Answer out loud if you can. You'll hear which ones have something behind them.
> - Most questions won't land. A few will. That's the whole point of a list this long.
> - Once you have a couple of candidates, pressure-test them against the three tests in [What makes a college essay topic actually work](/blog/what-makes-a-college-essay-topic-work/).

Most brainstorming advice hands you one or two prompts and calls it a day: "what matters to you," "what makes you, you." Those questions are too broad to answer honestly. You either freeze or you reach for something generic, because the question doesn't point anywhere specific. In my experience, this is exactly where most students get stuck: not because they lack material, but because the prompt they were given is too wide to walk through.

The fix is narrower questions, not better ones. This list has 20 of them, split into four groups. None of them ask you to summarize your whole personality. Each one points at one small, concrete thing: a moment, a person, a belief, a habit. A narrow question does the work a broad one can't, because it gives your memory somewhere specific to go instead of asking it to search everywhere at once.

If you want the full process for turning an answer into an actual topic, [How to figure out what to write your college essay about](/blog/how-to-choose-college-essay-topic/) covers that end to end, from first material to a working draft. This post is just the raw material. Read through the questions once, then go back and actually answer the ones that catch on something.

## Moments you'd almost forgotten

1. What is the last thing you built, fixed, or organized that took way longer than it should have?
2. Describe a moment when you understood something before the adult in the room did.
3. What is a plan that fell apart in front of you, and what did you do in the fifteen minutes after?
4. What is something you were embarrassed about at the time that you would explain differently now?
5. What is a place you go when you need to think, and what do you actually do once you get there?

### People you notice

6. Who is someone you argue with often, and what is the argument actually about underneath the argument?
7. Who taught you something without trying to teach it to you?
8. What is a message, comment, or note from someone that you kept and reread more than once?
9. Describe someone you have watched closely without them knowing, and what you picked up from watching.
10. Who do you call for advice on exactly one kind of problem, and why that person for that problem?

### What you actually believe

11. What is something you believe that most of your friends don't?
12. What is something you have thought about for way longer than the topic seems to deserve?
13. What is something you keep defending even when agreeing would be easier?
14. What is an opinion you used to argue for with total confidence that you can't fully explain anymore?
15. What is something you believe about yourself that you are not fully sure is true?

### Habits and choices nobody assigned you

16. What is something you always do the same way, even though a faster way exists?
17. What do you default to ordering, wearing, or choosing, and when did that become automatic?
18. What is something you do only when nobody is around to see it?
19. What is a decision that looked small when you made it but changed what came after?
20. What is something you quit or walked away from, and what finally made you stop?

## How to work through this list

Go fast. Read a question, answer it in one or two sentences, move on. Don't stop to make the answer sound good. This is a draft of your thinking, not a draft of your essay, and nobody is grading it. Treat a weak answer as information, not failure: it just tells you that question wasn't the door in, and you move to the next one.

The point isn't to answer all 20 well. It's to notice which ones you kept talking after you answered. Star those, or circle them on paper if you're working by hand. A question that gets a flat one-line answer is done, cross it off. A question where your one-line answer turned into three more sentences without you planning that is worth a second look.

Saying your answers out loud works better than writing them, if you can manage it. Writing invites editing, and editing is exactly what you're trying to skip on a first pass. Talking doesn't give you time to polish, so what comes out is closer to what you actually think. If you're doing this out loud, a voice memo on your phone is enough. You don't need to listen back right away. You'll remember which answer surprised you.

If you go through this alone, set a timer. Twenty minutes is enough for all 20 questions if you're not editing yourself. If a question doesn't produce anything after ten seconds, skip it and come back later. Some of these will only make sense to you on a second pass, after an earlier question has loosened something up. It's also fine to spread the list across two sittings instead of one. A question that felt flat on Tuesday can open up on Thursday once you've stopped trying to force it.

You'll know a question landed because your answer won't stay in the form of the question. You'll answer "who taught you something without trying to teach it," and instead of naming a person, you'll start describing a specific afternoon, or a specific thing they said that you still repeat to yourself. That drift away from the literal question, toward a scene or a detail, is the signal you're looking for. It means the question found something that was already sitting there, waiting for a reason to come out.

## What to do with what you find

Once you have two or three candidates, stop brainstorming and start testing. A good answer to one of these questions is a lead, not a finished topic yet. It tells you where to dig, not what to write.

Run each candidate through the three tests covered in [What makes a college essay topic actually work](/blog/what-makes-a-college-essay-topic-work/): is it specific enough to be yours alone, is it actually something you lived rather than something you think sounds impressive, and is there room in it to reflect on rather than just report. A topic that fails one of those tests usually still has something useful in it. It just means you go back to the question that produced it and dig one layer deeper, instead of moving straight to an outline.

Don't throw out a candidate the first time it feels thin. A lot of real topics sound small when you first say them out loud, "I water my neighbor's plants when she travels" or "I still keep a running tally of every book I've quit halfway through," and only get interesting once you push on the why. The push is where the actual material shows up: what you were avoiding, what changed, what you noticed that nobody else in the room noticed. If a candidate survives one round of "why does that matter to you" without you shrugging, it's worth building on.

This is also where it helps to say your answers out loud to someone else, because you'll hear which parts you're rushing past. A parent or a friend who knows you well enough to say "wait, go back to that" is doing more for your essay at this stage than any outline template. They're not evaluating whether it's a good topic. They're just noticing where you slowed down.

If two or three candidates all pass the three tests, don't force yourself to pick one right away. Sit with all of them for a day or two before you draft. The one you keep thinking about without being asked to is usually the one to write. That's a different signal than which one sounds most impressive on paper, and it's the more reliable one.

Answering a list like this alone in your room is fine. Answering it out loud, with something that asks a real follow-up question, tends to get further faster. That is what a conversation with [Mascot](https://mascot-ai.com/app/talk-to-bear) does: you talk through your answers, and it follows up on the ones with something underneath them. It does not write the essay.

You don't need all 20 to work. You need one.
